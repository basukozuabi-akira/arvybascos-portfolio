About

Fifteen years in the classroom, and the tools to show for it

Most classroom software is made by people who have never had to teach a lesson with it. I have — for fifteen years, across public, private and international school systems and every grade from Kinder to Senior High.

I am a licensed teacher with fifteen years of experience, and I have taught every level from Kinder to Senior High. I spent three years in a private school teaching elementary through high school, followed by seven years in the Philippine public school system teaching junior and senior high school. I then taught for three years at an international school in Okinawa, which sponsored my Montessori training. I am now in my second year teaching at a middle school international school in Okinawa, Japan.

That range is the reason I design the way I do. Teaching Kinder teaches you that nothing is obvious. Teaching Senior High teaches you how much scaffolding you can safely remove. Moving between a public school class of forty and an international classroom teaches you which methods survive a change of context and which quietly depended on ideal conditions. I currently work from the Abeka and Enlightium curricula across Algebra, Geometry, Science, English, US History and World History — and teaching several subjects at once has forced me to work out, lesson by lesson, what actually makes a concept land and what merely looks like teaching.

The building started out of necessity. I wanted practice that corrected itself while the student was still thinking, lessons that held a class's attention on a screen at the front of the room, and a way to stop a student from clicking past a section they had not understood. Nothing available did those things the way I needed, so I learned to make them — one self-contained HTML file at a time, no dependencies, working on whatever machine the school happens to have.

That work has since grown past my own classroom. I built and maintain a live WordPress site for Maranatha Baptist Church Okinawa, and I designed, coded and branded a full school LMS on PHP and MySQL — live synchronized lessons, attendance, assessments and official report cards. Different problems, the same habit: understand what the person on the other side actually needs, then build the smallest thing that delivers it.

I hold a Bachelor of Secondary Education, major in Technology and Livelihood Education. TLE is a subject about making things — teaching students to plan, build and troubleshoot practical work — and I did not expect it to become the foundation of everything on this page, but it is. It is why building software to solve a classroom problem felt like an extension of my teaching rather than a departure from it.

I am also Montessori trained. The international school where I taught for three years sponsored this professional training; the school itself was not a Montessori school. Montessori is built on a single idea I keep returning to: prepare the environment properly and the learner will teach themselves. That principle is the reason my materials correct themselves instead of waiting for me, why practice is placed between concepts instead of piled at the end, and why My Writing Journey guides a student through drafting without ever writing the sentence for them. The pedagogy came first; the code exists to deliver it.

I am fluent in spoken and written English, and Tagalog is my native language as a Filipino. I am now looking to bring fifteen years of classroom judgement to a team full-time — in instructional design, e-learning development or curriculum writing. I am comfortable owning something end to end, from a curriculum brief to a shipped, tested artifact.

Interactive English lessons

The problem
Grammar rules read off a textbook page don't stick. Students need to meet each rule one at a time, try it immediately, and get told whether they were right — before the next rule arrives.
What I built
A single self-contained HTML lesson per topic across Grammar, Literature, and Vocabulary & Spelling — styled to present full-screen on a classroom TV: large responsive type, arrow-key and dot navigation, tap-to-reveal rule cards, and checked practice between every subtopic. The wrap-up slide stays locked until every other slide has been visited.
Skills
Lesson sequencing from a curriculum plan, formative assessment design, responsive typography, keyboard accessibility, design-system consistency across a lesson series.

My Writing Journey

The problem
Writing is the one skill a teacher cannot scale. Thirty students each need feedback on a different draft, and the feedback only helps while they're still writing.
What I built
A self-guided writing coach with two levels. Beginners build sentences from word banks and chain them into a paragraph using guided connectors. Independent writers move through prompt, model, brainstorm, outline, draft, then write alone — with live criteria checks running as they type: word count, tense consistency, person, subordinate clauses, conjunctions, and parts of speech.
Skills
Scaffolded pedagogy, Montessori-informed self-direction, learner-progression modelling, real-time text parsing in JavaScript, feedback that guides without correcting for the student.

How I turn a curriculum module into a lesson

The problem
Students click through a slide deck without absorbing it, then hit the assessment cold. Nothing in a normal deck stops them from moving on before they're ready.
What I built
A repeatable process, not a one-off. Every lesson on this page came out of it — across math, science, English, US History and World History. One file each, no dependencies, works on any school machine.
Skills
Mastery-based progression, working faithfully from a published curriculum, templating a workflow so it can be repeated for any chapter or subject, copyright-aware content authoring.

The process, start to finish

  1. Read the day plan. Build only what this lesson covers — never borrow from a later one.
  2. Verify every source page. If a rule in the plan has no source behind it, I ask rather than invent the wording.
  3. Write the teaching slides. One rule per reveal, original examples, nothing skipped.
  4. Place practice between subtopics. Sorting, typed answers, multiple choice — checkpoints throughout, not one at the end.
  5. Lock the wrap-up. It stays closed until every slide has been visited.
